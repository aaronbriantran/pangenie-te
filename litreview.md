The control of the context and level of gene expression via variation in chromatin structure is critical in establishing cell type and function (Gibney and Nolan, 2010; Holliday, 2006) and maintaining cellular regulatory networks (Klemm et al., 2019). I define chromatin structure as any chemical or topological feature of chromatin excluding the sequence of nucleotide bases.

Chromatin structure is established through several known mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and cohesin loops (Gibney and Nolan, 2010; Nolan et al., 2024). Variations in these chromatin structural components modulate the effect of the genetic code on overall phenotype by modifying the context and degree of gene expression and, therefore, protein synthesis.

To affect the collective properties of a multicellular system, these variations must be taken up by a large population of cells, either by persisting through multiple rounds of mitosis or meiosis or by arising simultaneously across many cells. I identify three sources of collective chromatin structure variation. Shared environmental exposures may induce similar chromatin states in an entire cell population. Alternately, chromatin states may propagate by being directly replicated in daughter cells from a parent cell (Stewart-Morgan et al., 2020). These heritable chromatin states encompass the epigenome (Deans and Maggert, 2015). Finally, genetic variations may cause sequence-dependent chromatin structural variations that reestablish themselves in daughter cells that bear the same causal genetic variants.

There are multiple mechanisms by which environmental exposures can modify chromatin states. For example, signal molecules and transcription factors (Basson, 2012) initiate the process of cell differentiation through a series of predictable, increasingly specific, and irreversible transitions between different epigenetic states which in turn modulate functions critical to different cell types (Morris et al., 2016; Waddington, 1942). Outside of the human body, exposure to toxins, UV radiation, and social influences, among others, have been shown to induce chromatin state variations (Stein, 2012).

Strictly defined, the epigenome corresponds to heritable chromatin state changes that are not sequence-dependent. A notable example of epigenetic inheritance of chromatin variation that affects expression includes parent-of-origin effects, in which differential methylation causes unbalanced expression across alleles depending on which parent provided a given allele (Lawson et al., 2013). Direct replication of chromatin features also preserves variations across mitotic divisions in a phenomenon known as cell memory (Stewart-Morgan et al., 2020). However, minimal evidence exists for meaningful epigenetic transmission of chromatin features transgenerationally across meiotically derived gametes, due in part to large-scale chromatin reprogramming during mammalian development (Fitz-James and Cavalli, 2022). Thus, epigenetic inheritance largely explains chromatin state variations somatically or only within one generation at imprinted genes susceptible to parent-of-origin effects.

Finally, chromatin variation may occur in a sequence-dependent manner. For example, Fragile X disorder is caused by knockout of expression of FMR1, which is caused by hypermethylation of the promoter in turn caused by an expansion of upstream CGG repeats (Pieretti et al., 1991).

Studying the impact of sequence variation on chromatin variation is a high priority because it is one of the best explanations of chromatin variation between individuals. Because transgenerational epigenetic inheritance is minimal, most chromatin variation must therefore be explained either by variations in environmental exposures (which cause chromatin state changes that may then propagate epigenetically) or DNA sequence. Furthermore, associating chromatin variations with genetic variations may help explain the effect of non-coding genetic variation on phenotypic traits by providing a mechanism by which these variants impact gene expression and regulation (GTEX Consortium, 2020).

Identifying instances of sequence-dependent regulatory variation across the genome currently involves either discovering expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) or performing allele specific (AS) analyses, which both reveal variants associated with differences in gene expression. By mapping chromatin variations to nearby variants linked with expression changes, we can identify likely chromatin state variations caused by genetic variation that modulate gene expression.

eQTL are variants that are discovered to be associated with changes in expression level of a gene in a large association study. These eQTL can narrow the possible sequences relevant to the expression level of a given gene to a specific region in linkage disequilibrium with each eQTL (Zhang and Zhao, 2023). On the other hand, AS analysis employs smaller numbers of individuals. If heterozygous at a given locus, RNA-seq reads can be assigned to one haplotype or another and compared for expression differences. Compared to eQTL studies, which combine data from individuals with variable environmental exposures, AS studies are more resistant to confounding by non-genetic effects on chromatin state, as expression is directly compared to the opposing haplotype in the same individual (Tycko, 2010). Furthermore, although AS analysis does not identify possible regulatory regions, it can be combined with eQTL-identified regions to do so (Cleary and Seoighe, 2021). Note, however, that AS analysis can only identify expression regulation that occurs in *cis,* although the majority of eQTLs in humans are cis-acting *(*Ge et al., 2009; Michaelson et al., 2009; Taudt et al., 2016).

An alternative means of controlling for external confounders is through the use of human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). iPSCs are created by the artificial expression of Yamanaka factors, which revert somatic cells to an embryo-like state (Takahashi and Yamanaka, 2006). Since embryonic stem cells undergo epigenetic reprogramming and correspond to a specific stage of cell identity, iPSCs likely control for epigenetic variation due to environment and cell fate (Banovich et al., 2018). Yet, iPSCs also recover donor-dependent effects on the epigenome, suggesting that a significant proportion of epigenetic variation in iPSCs can be attributed to preserved genetic effects (DeBoever et al., 2017; Kyttälä et al., 2016).

Because of the advantages iPSCs have as a model of chromatin-genetic interactions, multiple eQTL studies have been conducted in iPSCs, collated in Bonder et al.’s (2021) survey of the field. Integrating data from the HipSci, iPSCORE, and Banovich et al. groups among others, they find 18,340 genes with at least one cis eQTL. However, the field notably suffers a lack of AS studies in iPSCs. As described above, AS analysis is better controlled than eQTL studies, and in conjunction are effective at locating genetic causes of chromatin variation (Zhang and Zhao, 2013). Additionally, iPSCs remove sources of random allelic expression that are epigenetically induced, thus increasing sensitivity to allele imbalance due to genetic variation (Jeffries et al., 2016). Similarly, there is a dearth of QTL studies that use accessibility or methylation in iPSCs, and fewer allele specific accessiblity or methylation studies still.

Some of the constituent studies of the Bonder et al. dataset do attempt AS analyses. Carcamo-Orive et al. (2017) looked at allele imbalance only for canonically imprinted genes, finding that a proportion of these canonically imprinted genes do not display imbalance in iPSCs. DeBoever et al. (2017) find that allele imbalance is affected by SNPs in accessible promoters and copy number variation. In an unrelated study, Zhang et al. (2020) uncover allele-specific accessible chromatin in neural cells derived from iPSCs that correspond to SNPs associated with neurodevelopmental disorders. No study, however, has performed AS analysis genome-wide and across multiple transcriptomic data types.

AS analyses can suffer from reference bias, or the tendency of reads containing non-reference alleles to map less frequently to reference sequences. Therefore, transcriptomic reads over a heterozygous reference/non-reference locus may spuriously indicate underexpression of the alternate allele in an AS analysis, especially as the variant gets larger. Pangenomes can be used to infer these personal, non-reference alleles and significantly reduce reference bias (Lin et al., 2024). Therefore, pangenomes are most likely to improve performance of AS analyses across large structural variants, which are most likely to suffer from reference bias. Pangenome inference tools such as Pangenie demonstrate strong performance in genotyping structural variants from pangenome graphs (Ebler et al., 2022). These tools could thus alleviate reference bias and recover rare structural variants, which are the major drivers of cis-regulatory chromatin state variation (Chiang et al., 2017).

This study thus has two motivations. Firstly, a genome-wide, allele-specific analysis of expression and chromatin state differences in iPSCs is a precise, sample-efficient way of recovering heritable sequence dependent chromatin state variation. Secondly, leveraging pangenome methods decreases reference biases in AS analysis and improve the recovery of structural variants, which are highly relevant in cis-regulatory variation.

To address this gap, I leverage data from Quaid et al. (2025), incorporating ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, WGBS, and whole genome sequencing data from iPSCs derived from a father/daughter donor pair and an unrelated donor line. This combined allele-specific and iPSC-based approach best minimizes environmental confounders in measuring sequence-dependent regulatory variation. Furthermore, because of the inclusion of methylation and accessibility data, this approach can examine allele imbalances in expression, accessibility, and methylation. Thus, this study extends allele-specific analysis and the idea of allele imbalances to all chromatin state variations in general, which can now be directly linked to nearby genetic variation. Finally, because this data contains related donors, I can also investigate how consistently inherited genetic variants in the daughter reproduce an associated chromatin state from the father. That is, how responsible is genetic heritability for the heritability of chromatin landscapes?

Thus, I aim to investigate the proximal effect of cis-genetic variation on epigenetic features, namely methylation and chromatin accessibility through an allele-specific analysis of WGBS, ATAC-seq, WGS, and bulk RNA-seq data derived from a culture of hiPSCs derived from a father/daughter donor pair and an unrelated donor line.
